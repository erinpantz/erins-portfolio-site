### Where I came into the picture

Laguna initially offered care management services with its own clinical staff and software. When AI became viable to build on, they saw a chance to deliver a lightweight co-pilot to increase efficiency with minimal disruption of existing workflows. I joined in January 2024 and collaborated with the product team based in Israel. When their UX designer left that April, I took over the MVP design.

<Section heading="Testing call prep and real-time features">

<TextBlock heading="What I tested for">

### The IDOH Flower

The IDOH Flower was intended to give case managers a 360° view of a member’s “Individual Determinants of Health.” Using progressive disclosure, we would indicate member needs across 6 categories and the user could drill down for more info. I chose a flower to reflect the nurturing nature of the care manager.

  <UnorderedList>
    <UnorderedListItem title="Simplify member details">To simplify the member info tab, we removed barriers, since duplicating what the existing CRM content showed added little value.</UnorderedListItem>
    <UnorderedListItem title="The flower needed clarity">I added labels and redesigned how statuses were presented. I also added a list view per user request.</UnorderedListItem>
    <UnorderedListItem title="Numbering implied clinical judgment">Users were concerned about the AI overriding their judgment, so I dropped the ordering of items.</UnorderedListItem>

<Section heading="Design system">

When I started on the product screens, Laguna’s components were scattered across different Figma files and pages. It was hard to find the latest version of anything, or to know whether we already had a component we could reuse for a new feature. All we had for a system was a small style guide for color and type.

### A central Figma library

Together with the R&D team, I chose Material Design as our base and built one central library for all of our styles and components. Then, I added pages for custom components. It became the hub for product design: easier to find what already existed, easier to add new pieces, and a smoother handoff to engineers.

<Section heading="Final designs">

<TextBlock heading="Call prep">

Before Laguna, case managers pieced together a member’s story from many different places. Call prep put it in one place they could glance at to get up to speed quickly.

<UnorderedList>
  <UnorderedListItem title="Case summary">A narrative, at-a-glance overview of the member’s case, updated automatically after every conversation. Testers reached for it first, so it moved to the top of the screen.</UnorderedListItem>
  <UnorderedListItem title="IDOH Flower">A categorized view of the member’s traits that shows areas of risk, non-issues, and unknowns to guide the next conversation. After testing, I added labels and clearer status colors, plus a list view for anyone who preferred it.</UnorderedListItem>
  <UnorderedListItem title="Suggested actions">Tasks recommended for the next conversation, based on the last one. I removed their numbering after testers said it felt like the AI was telling them how to do their job.</UnorderedListItem>
</UnorderedList>

</TextBlock>

### Live call

With so much to cover in a call, it’s easy to miss a key topic. The SmartPad sends gentle nudges when something comes up that should be addressed. Early versions showed too much at once, so I simplified the live view to the SmartPad alone, with the transcript and prep notes one click away.

The live transcript turned out to be more helpful than we expected: when a member had a strong accent or a speech impediment, or when a case manager just wanted to look back at what was said. It uncovered benefits of Laguna Companion we had underestimated.

<TextBlock heading="Post-call">

Before Laguna, documenting a call took about as long as the call itself, and feedback depended on a supervisor finding time to listen in. Post-call took care of both as soon as the case manager hung up.

<UnorderedList>
  <UnorderedListItem title="Post-call summary">A call note generated about a minute after the call, in a compliant format case managers could copy straight into their records. It was the biggest time saver in their workflow, and it kept getting faster: Laguna learned from the edits case managers made and adjusted the model over time.</UnorderedListItem>
  <UnorderedListItem title="Post-call feedback">Personalized feedback on each conversation, highlighting what went well and what to try next time. Supervisors rarely had time to listen to every call and coach each person, so this gave case managers a way to keep growing between those conversations.</UnorderedListItem>
</UnorderedList>

</TextBlock>

<Section heading="Launching and learning on site">

We launched in October 2024 with 5 champion users, then 22 more two weeks later. Staging the rollout let early adopters surface issues and become go-to resources for their teammates. The response was strong enough that the health plan invited us onsite.

In November, I spent three days shadowing case managers. A lot was working: they got up to speed quickly, the post-call notes were high quality, and they told me they felt more present with members. Post-call feedback was a standout.

<Quote
  quote="It’s my favorite screen. It’s like my cheerleader… even if it’s just an AI system, it’s my cheerleader and it keeps me going."
  name="Case Manager"
/>

I also saw what needed work. The case summary’s expanded view was hard to find, editing felt cramped next to the wide Word document they were used to, and our installation flow broke under the health plan’s strict IT policies.

Over the next few months, we shipped fixes: a wider editing mode that opens automatically, a case summary that starts expanded, an installation flow the health plan could manage, and versions of the post-call note tailored to each clinical team’s documentation style.

<Section heading="What I learned">

<UnorderedList>
  <UnorderedListItem title="Good design isn’t enough if the AI content isn’t good.">Every UX fundamental I’d relied on for a decade still applied, but none of it mattered if the summary missed the mark or the real-time nudges were noise instead of signal. That’s a different kind of design problem, and I learned it as we went.</UnorderedListItem>
  <UnorderedListItem title="“Just make it a copilot” undersells how hard the mundane parts are.">Getting a case manager to hit record at exactly the right moment, while introducing themselves and pulling up their CRM, took as much iteration as any headline feature, because getting it wrong broke trust in everything else.</UnorderedListItem>
  <UnorderedListItem title="Deep customization built trust, but it came at a real cost.">Tailoring the product to each team’s documentation needs set us apart and was a big part of why users loved it. It also asked a lot of our customer: several rounds of review to get to “good.” I still think about how to reach that bar with less upfront effort from the people we’re serving.</UnorderedListItem>
</UnorderedList>

</Section>
